# Process overview

## What I built

**Democracy?** is a satirical multiplayer parliament. A table of citizens elects a Presenter each round, the Presenter tables one bill, the table votes, and a pass is permanent and visible to everyone watching. No accounts beyond a claimed name, no admin role, ever.

## How I worked

Three projects led to this one. Crits 8–10 ran against an earlier app, a one-number headcount for one pod, and left three lessons: scope narrow enough that "good" stops being abstract ([`cf7a850`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/cf7a850)), prove a claim by running the thing rather than reading the code ([`033ed04`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/033ed04)), and authorization lives in a `WHERE` clause the server enforces, not an `if` the UI shows ([`751617a`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/751617a)). All three carried into **Democracy?**: Rule 1's guarded write is crit 9's never-trust-a-client-count instinct applied to round resolution, Rule 4's `presenter_id` check in the `WHERE` clause is crit 10's authorization pattern reused (both landed in [`47b377f`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/47b377f)), and "logs that name people" (below) is the same crit-10 lesson relearned after it got dropped across two pivots ([`cd36d64`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/cd36d64)). Two restarts followed and got dropped: an air connected Canberra, where I couldn't model the air physics convincingly ([`f319211`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/f319211)), and ComplyLogic, a compliance tracker I abandoned over an unresolved product-placement question ([`d79ecc4`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/d79ecc4)). 

I then pivoted to a parliamentary simulator. The agent's first plan kept a chat-log format with an LLM "Constitution Bot" narrating each bill's passage. I sent it back twice: citizens should sit at a round table, declare themselves Presenter candidates, and the table votes them in directly ("remove the llm bot, people are the bill presenters"), black-and-white, with theme advice like black and white pixels and stand-up animation on election. Named **Democracy?** once that shape was agreed ([`47b377f`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/47b377f)). CLAUDE.md's eight non-negotiables and the stack, Node 24 with no dependencies, carrying over ComplyLogic's guarded-write and SSE patterns, landed in that same first commit. Rule 1 (a round resolves inside one guarded write) and Rule 3 (no override path, enforced by trigger in `db.ts`) are both traced back to a clause of README's claim rather than inferred after the fact.

## The moments that mattered

1. A crit 10 lesson had to be relearned. What happened: an audit turned up log lines carrying a raw citizen id, and "citizen 7 declared" meant nothing without a lookup table, the exact mistake crit 10's authorization work had already taught me to avoid once. What I did: `event()` in `game.ts` now writes the citizen's name into the log line, and bills got a sequential `bill_no` so the same bill can be pointed to on the page, in the minutes, and in the log ([`cd36d64`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/cd36d64)). How I knew: I tried to narrate a round from the logs alone and couldn't tell citizens apart. This was my main goal on transparency, people should be able to see the voting track record of others and know what policies they stand for.

2. The tie-break had to be decided one specific way, not the easy way. What happened: the election and the bill vote both need a rule for a tie, and `Math.random()` was the obvious choice, less code, nothing to defend. What I did: rejected it in `decisions/002`, because a random tie-break reopens the one question the whole app exists to make unaskable, whether the result was rigged; earliest-declarer-wins is boring, but a citizen can check it themselves against the order they declared in, so it became Rule 6 rather than a convenience left to chance ([`47b377f`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/47b377f)). How I knew it mattered enough to lock in: it's the one rule where the alternative isn't just worse engineering, it's a decision someone could reasonably accuse the app of rigging.

3. CLAUDE.md only covered testing for parts that already existed. What happened: I noticed the working agreements described what had been tested, not what should be tested going forward. What I did: asked for "a rule in claude.md for adding test in spec/ for all deterministic parts," now CLAUDE.md's closing working agreement ([`cd36d64`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/cd36d64)). How I knew: checking which existing modules had no `spec/*.test.ts` file sitting next to them.

4. Selecting text on the page kept breaking, and the fix became a rule, not a patch. What happened: the server pushes a snapshot every second to keep countdowns honest, and the client rebuilt both the Bills Passed and Bills Failed lists on every push, regardless of whether anything had actually changed, which collapsed whatever a reader had selected mid-click. What I did: fixed it by comparing a cheap signature, length plus newest id, against what was already rendered and skipping the rebuild when it hadn't moved, then wrote that pattern into CLAUDE.md as a working agreement rather than leaving it as something only `app.js` remembers ([`cd36d64`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/cd36d64)). How I knew: it happened every single time I tried to select anything on the page while a round was running, not just occasionally.

5. Whether an empty chamber reads as satire or as a malfunction isn't something a test can check. What happened: the mechanism was airtight, but I still didn't know if it read as the joke it was meant to be. What I did: seated people at the table and watched. How I knew: an uncontested win landed as the joke, not as something that looked broken.

6. Who gets to see a citizen's vote was a call I almost made the quiet way. What happened: hiding live vote counts from everyone but the candidates, and leaving the full record as something only an operator running `flyctl logs` could pull up, looked like a reasonable guard against tactical voting, and it was less to build. What I did: rejected that in `decisions/005`, because README's claim is that the procedure is visible to everyone as it happens; a tally a convenor could narrate from logs but a citizen couldn't see on the page doesn't protect that claim, it just splits the table into who can check the record and who can't ([`cd36d64`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-manavs7782612/commit/cd36d64)). How I knew: the whole point of "live for everyone" fails if the one place it's actually visible is a server log the people voting don't have access to.
